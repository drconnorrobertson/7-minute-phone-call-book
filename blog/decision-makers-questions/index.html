Questions That Separate Conversation Roles from Decision Authority

Clarify who supplied information, who can review a proposal and which next steps have actually been accepted in an acquisition conversation.

Published 2026-09-23 · Updated 2026-10-30By The 7-Minute Phone Call Editorial

A person who can explain a business may have a different role from a person who can accept a proposal. Before turning a conversation into a decision record, ask which specific action is being discussed and what the participant says their role covers. This question set helps prepare that conversation; it does not determine legal authority or validate an ownership record.

The prompts below are original examples, not book quotations, recorded calls or tested sales scripts. Use a prompt for an actual unresolved point. A confident answer, senior job title or introduction by another participant does not by itself resolve every question about the next action.

Name the action before asking who decides

“Are we discussing permission to send an overview, a request for a particular record or a proposal to change a transaction term?” separates three different requests. A person may be able to arrange the first conversation while another person handles a later review. Write down the action in the participant's words before using a broad label such as “decision maker.”

For example, an invitation to send a short recap establishes a proposed communication step. It does not establish acceptance of the recap's conclusions, permission to share every attachment or agreement to a price. Keep each request separate when asking who should be involved.

Ask about the participant's stated role

“Which part of this discussion are you handling, and which part would need someone else's review?” invites a description of scope. Follow with “Is that your current role for this particular request?” if a participant describes a past transaction or a usual process. A previous arrangement may be useful context without establishing the arrangement for the present conversation.

Record the source of the description. “The participant said they coordinate information requests” is different from “the participant can approve a transaction.” Do not silently replace a narrow description with a broader one. If the scope remains unclear, retain the question in the notes.

Separate information, advice and acceptance

Ask “Who prepared this figure, who can explain its definition and who would review a proposal based on it?” These roles may belong to different people. Knowing the source of a number does not establish that the source endorses a proposed use of it. An advisor's comment also does not establish that another participant has accepted a recommendation.

If a participant says a colleague should be consulted, ask what the consultation is expected to cover. “Review the schedule” should not become “approve all terms” in the recap. Keep the question, the person named and any unanswered scope visible together.

Clarify an introduction before treating it as a handoff

“Would you prefer to introduce us, or should this question remain with you for now?” leaves the communication path open. If an introduction is proposed, ask which topic should be included and which materials, if any, are appropriate to share. Being given a name is not the same as receiving an introduction or an accepted assignment.

Before writing that a colleague owns a follow-up, ask whether that person has accepted it. A statement such as “I will ask Morgan whether they can review the summary” records a request to be made. It does not record Morgan's acceptance, a completed review or a confirmed date.

Check the meaning of an apparent agreement

“When you say this looks reasonable, do you mean the summary reflects our conversation, or that you are accepting the proposed step?” gives the speaker room to distinguish acknowledgment from agreement. Preserve any conditions the person adds. A response subject to another review should remain subject to that review in the notes.

If the action needs a form of authorization or supporting record, identify that as an unresolved requirement for the appropriate review. Do not use this prompt list to decide what documentation is legally sufficient. No company, ownership document, signature or actual transaction has been examined here.

Close with a recap that can be corrected

A bounded recap might say: “You said you coordinate information requests. You will ask whether Morgan can review the draft schedule. Morgan has not accepted a task or date. Is that accurate?” This preserves what was said while making the gaps easy to correct. The names and situation in this example are fictional.

Keep a proposed next step separate from a confirmed one. If no response arrives, record that the question remains unanswered rather than assigning a reason. For related preparation, see questions for clarifying a follow-up and prompts that leave room for a correction.

Common questions

Does a job title establish authority for every action?

This guide does not determine authority. Ask about the specific action and the participant’s stated role, and keep any required review or documentation unresolved until it is addressed.

Is a source of information also the person accepting a proposal?

Do not assume those roles are the same. Record who supplied information and separately ask who would review or accept the proposed action.

Does naming a colleague create an accepted assignment?

No acceptance has been established by the name alone. Distinguish a suggested contact, an introduction, a request and the colleague’s actual response.

How should a conditional response appear in the recap?

Retain the condition and identify what remains unanswered. An acknowledgment subject to another review should not be recorded as unconditional acceptance.
