The Discovery Call Done Right: How to Ask Questions That Make Prospects Sell Themselves

The best salespeople talk less than you think. Here is the questioning framework that gets prospects to articulate their own pain -- and ask you for the solution.

By Dr. Connor Robertson · July 8, 2026 · 7 min read

Most salespeople spend their discovery calls pitching. They spend the first ten minutes asking a few surface questions and then spend the next twenty explaining their product. They think they are building value. What they are actually doing is talking themselves out of deals they could have closed.

The discovery call is not a precursor to your pitch. It is the most important sales conversation you will ever have with a prospect, and its primary purpose is not for you to speak at all. It is to get the prospect to speak -- specifically, to articulate the exact problem they have, in their own words, with enough emotional specificity that they have essentially made the case for your solution before you have said a word about it.

When discovery is done right, the close is not a persuasion act. It is a natural conclusion that both people arrive at together.

Why Most Discovery Calls Fail

There are two failure modes that kill most discovery calls. The first is going too shallow. You ask "What are you struggling with?" and you get a generic answer -- "pipeline," "conversion," "team bandwidth" -- and you accept that answer and move into your pitch. You now have a surface-level understanding of a surface-level problem, and any solution you offer is going to feel generic because it was built on generic input.

The second failure mode is going too fast. The salesperson hears a problem, recognizes it as something they can solve, and immediately pivots into solution mode. "Oh, that is exactly what we help with -- let me tell you how." The prospect has not had the chance to sit with the weight of the problem. They have not fully articulated what it is costing them. The emotional readiness to buy has not built up yet, and your solution lands in a vacuum.

Effective discovery solves both problems through a simple discipline: you ask more questions, you go deeper on each answer, and you do not pivot to solutions until the prospect has fully expressed the scope and cost of their situation.

The Four Layers of Discovery

Think of a good discovery call as moving through four layers. Each layer takes you closer to the core of why this prospect needs to make a change now -- and why they need to make it with you.

The first layer is situational. Where are things right now? What does their current setup look like? These are not particularly insightful questions on their own, but they establish context and they get the prospect talking. "Walk me through what your current process looks like" is a better version of "tell me about your business" because it asks for something specific and concrete.

The second layer is problem. What is not working? What are the friction points? This is where most salespeople stop digging, and it is a mistake. Generic problem statements lead to generic solutions. "Our close rate is lower than we want" is the beginning of a problem, not a complete articulation of one. Your job is to understand the anatomy of the problem -- when does it happen, what triggers it, who is affected, how long has it been going on.

The third layer is impact. This is where discovery does its real work. Once you know what the problem is, you need to understand what it is costing the prospect -- in dollars, in time, in stress, in missed opportunities, in team morale. The impact layer is where problems become urgent. "Our close rate is low" is a chronic condition people live with. "Our close rate is low, which means we are burning through leads that cost us $400 each and leaving roughly $60,000 a month on the table" is a burning platform that demands action.

The fourth layer is implication. What happens if nothing changes? Where does this problem lead in six months, a year, three years? The implication layer helps the prospect see that inaction is itself a choice -- and often the most expensive one. It also shifts the conversation from "should we solve this" to "how urgently do we need to solve this."

The Questions That Go Deeper

Moving through these layers is not about having a rigid script. It is about developing the habit of going one question deeper than your first instinct tells you to. Here are the follow-up patterns that do it consistently.

"Tell me more about that." The simplest and most underused prompt in sales. When a prospect gives you an answer, this phrase signals that you are genuinely interested and not just collecting data points to justify your pitch. It produces more context almost every time.

"What does that look like in practice?" Abstract problems are hard to sell against. Concrete ones are not. When a prospect says their team is "not aligned," ask them what that looks like in practice -- what specifically happens, in what situations, between which people. You want the movie, not the summary.

"How long has this been the case?" Duration matters. A problem that has existed for three months feels different than one that has existed for three years. The longer something has persisted, the more evidence there is that it will not solve itself -- and the more useful it is to name that explicitly.

"What have you tried so far?" This question does two things. It shows you are not going to recommend something they have already rejected, and it surfaces the prospect's relationship with the problem. People who have tried multiple things and failed are more motivated to find a real solution than people who have not yet engaged with the problem at all.

"What would it mean for you personally if this were solved?" This is the question that moves the conversation from business impact to personal stake. Decisions are emotional at their core, even in B2B. When someone connects solving a problem to their own peace of mind, career, or sense of professional competence, the emotional case for buying is made -- and they made it themselves.

The goal of discovery is not to collect information. It is to help the prospect understand their own situation more clearly than they did before they talked to you. When that happens, you become the most useful person in the room -- and the natural next step is working with you.

The Listening Posture That Makes All of This Work

You can have all the right questions and still run a bad discovery call if your listening posture is wrong. The most common version of this is what I call "listening to respond" -- you ask a question, the prospect starts answering, and before they finish you are already formulating your next move. You are technically present but not actually absorbing what is being said.

Effective discovery requires what might be called listening to understand. Your only goal while the prospect is speaking is to understand what they are saying with as much precision as possible. You are not planning your response. You are not looking for the keyword that lets you launch into your pitch. You are building a complete mental picture of their situation so that your response -- when you give one -- is actually responsive rather than pre-packaged.

In practice, this means being comfortable with a beat of silence after a prospect finishes speaking. That pause is not empty. It communicates that you processed what they said before reacting to it, which is something that almost no one does in a sales context. It builds trust. And more often than not, the prospect uses that silence to add something -- often the most important thing they have said so far.

When to Transition Out of Discovery

One of the things that trips up even experienced salespeople is knowing when to stop discovering and start connecting the dots. There are two signals to watch for.

The first is completeness. Have you heard enough to fully understand the problem, its cost, and its urgency? Can you articulate their situation back to them in a way that would make them feel genuinely seen? If yes, you are ready to transition. If there are still gaps, keep asking.

The second signal is energy. Has the prospect reached the point where they are leaning in? Are they talking faster, getting more specific, expressing frustration or urgency? That is the moment when their emotional readiness to hear a solution is highest, and it is the right time to bridge from discovery into what is possible. "Based on what you have described, let me show you how we typically approach this for people in exactly your situation."

Notice the framing: you are not pivoting to a pitch. You are connecting what you just heard to what you do. The prospect does not experience this as a gear shift. It feels like the natural continuation of a conversation that was always about their situation.

The Discovery Mindset

Everything above is technique. Technique matters. But underneath the technique, the thing that makes the best discovery callers so effective is a genuine belief that their job on this call is not to sell -- it is to understand.

When you are actually curious about a prospect's situation, your questions are better. Your follow-ups are more natural. You listen more carefully because you actually want to know the answer. The prospect can feel the difference between someone who is working through a script and someone who is genuinely interested in understanding their problem.

That distinction is what separates salespeople who close at 20% from those who close at 50%. It is not a better pitch. It is a better conversation.

The 7-minute framework is built around this idea. When you are disciplined about discovery -- when you ask the right questions, listen for the real answers, and let the prospect articulate their own need -- the pitch becomes almost secondary. The work was done in the questions.
