The Voicemail Formula: How to Leave a Message That Gets Called Back

Most sales voicemails are deleted in 3 seconds. Here is what to say instead.

By Dr. Connor Robertson · June 24, 2026 · 7 min read

Your prospect did not pick up. Now you have about four seconds to decide whether to hang up or leave a message. Most salespeople leave a message. Almost all of them do it wrong.

The average sales voicemail lasts 60 to 90 seconds. It covers the caller's name, their company, their product, their value proposition, their contact information, and a vague request to "give me a call back when you get a chance." That voicemail gets deleted before the prospect even hears your name a second time.

The problem is not that voicemails do not work. The problem is that most salespeople treat a voicemail like a mini pitch instead of what it actually is: a pattern interrupt designed to earn a callback.

Here is how to fix it.

The 22-Second Rule

The most effective sales voicemails run between 18 and 22 seconds. That is not a suggestion -- it is a constraint you should treat as non-negotiable. When your message hits the 22-second mark and stops, it does something powerful: it leaves the prospect with a sense of incompleteness. Their brain wants resolution. That psychological pull is what drives callbacks.

Anything longer than 22 seconds dilutes that effect and starts to feel like a pitch. Your prospect stops listening and starts calculating how quickly they can delete it.

Time your voicemails before you dial. Write your script. Practice it. Know that when you get to voicemail, you are going to say exactly those words in exactly that order and hang up at 22 seconds or less.

The Four-Part Formula

Every effective sales voicemail has four components, delivered in this order:

1. Your name and one context anchor. Give your first name and one specific detail that tells them why you are calling. Not your company. Not your title. Just a single anchor that makes you sound like someone who did their homework. "Hi, this is Connor -- I was looking at your company's recent expansion into the Nashville market."

2. A relevant trigger. Something is happening in their world that made you reach out. Name it. This is the difference between sounding like every other cold caller and sounding like someone worth talking to. The trigger can be an industry trend, a company announcement, a role change, or even a common challenge you have seen in their space. "I have been speaking with a lot of regional directors in your industry who are navigating the same distribution headache right now."

3. A specific result, not a feature. Do not tell them what you do. Tell them what happens for people in their situation who work with you. Keep it to one sentence. "The teams I work with typically cut that problem in half within the first 60 days." That is it. Do not elaborate.

4. A time-specific callback request. Do not say "call me back when you get a chance." That phrasing signals low priority. Instead, give them a specific window. "I will be available this afternoon between 2 and 4, or tomorrow morning. My number is [number]." Say the number once, clearly, without rushing. Do not repeat it.

Put those four parts together and your voicemail sounds like this:

"Hi, this is Connor -- I came across your profile while researching CFOs navigating the new reporting requirements this quarter. I have been working with a handful of finance leaders in your space, and most of them are dealing with the same compliance timing issue. The ones I have connected with have been able to get ahead of it in about three weeks. I would love to share how. I am available Thursday between 1 and 3, or Friday morning. My number is 555-0100."

That message is under 22 seconds. It is specific. It does not pitch. It does not beg. And it gives the prospect a reason to call back that has nothing to do with you selling something.

The Tone That Earns Callbacks

You can have the perfect script and still kill it with the wrong delivery. The most common tone mistake in sales voicemails is sounding either too eager or too apologetic. Both signal desperation, and desperation kills curiosity.

What earns callbacks is confident neutrality. You are not excited to leave this message. You are not nervous about whether they will call back. You are simply a peer with relevant information, letting them know you exist. Speak at a conversational pace. Smile slightly when you talk -- it changes the sound of your voice in a detectable way. Do not slow down on your phone number as if you are leaving a voicemail for your grandmother. Say it at a normal pace, clearly.

Record yourself before you actually dial. Listen back. Does it sound like someone you would want to call back? If not, adjust.

The Voicemail Plus Email Sequence

A voicemail alone has a low callback rate. A voicemail followed immediately by a matching email has a dramatically higher one. Within three minutes of leaving the voicemail, send a short email that references the message you just left. Subject line: "Just left you a voicemail." Body: two sentences mirroring what you said, and one sentence with a specific call-to-action.

This one-two sequence works because it shows up on two different surfaces -- the prospect's phone and their inbox -- within minutes of each other. It creates the impression of momentum without feeling like spam, as long as the message is consistent and the email is brief. Three sentences maximum. No attachments on the first touch.

When Not to Leave a Voicemail

There are situations where leaving a voicemail does more harm than good. If you have already left one voicemail in the same week and have not heard back, do not leave another one. Multiple voicemails without callbacks signal that your prospect is actively avoiding you, and leaving another message confirms that you are not picking up on that signal. Instead, switch to email or try at a different time of day on a different day of the week.

Also skip the voicemail if you have no specific context anchor for this prospect. A generic voicemail is worse than silence -- it actively tells your prospect that you did not do your homework. If you cannot find one specific, relevant thing to say, hang up and do ten minutes of research before you call back.

The Decision Tree After a Voicemail

Here is how to sequence your outreach after leaving a voicemail. Leave the voicemail. Send the follow-up email within three minutes. Wait 48 hours. If no response, send a second short email that asks a single yes-or-no question -- something like "Is this even on your radar for this quarter?" That question gets a higher response rate than any amount of follow-up pitching. Wait another 48 hours. If still no response, make one final call without leaving a voicemail. If no answer, move the prospect to a long-cycle nurture sequence and redirect your energy.

The voicemail is not a hail Mary. It is the first step in a structured sequence. Treat it like one.

The salespeople who get the most callbacks are not the ones with the best products. They are the ones who understand that every word they say -- even to an empty voicemail box -- is a reflection of how they think, how they prepare, and how seriously they take the other person's time. Get those 22 seconds right, and the other six minutes become a lot easier.
