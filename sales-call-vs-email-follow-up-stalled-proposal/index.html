Call vs Email Follow-Up for a Stalled Proposal

A stalled proposal does not automatically need another call or a longer email. Choose the channel by the uncertainty you need to resolve and the buyer's preferred way to communicate. The purpose of a follow-up is to clarify a decision, including a decision to stop, rather than to create activity for its own sake.

Work through the decision

  1. Name the unanswered question

    Write down what remains unknown: fit, scope, timing, participants or a specific approval. If you cannot name the question, do not send a generic checking-in message. Review the last conversation and distinguish what the buyer actually said from what you assumed after sending the proposal.

  2. Use email for a defined written answer

    A concise email can work when the buyer needs a corrected detail, a scope summary or an artifact to share internally. Put the open question near the top and make the requested response easy to understand. Avoid attaching a new presentation when the original question needs one sentence.

  3. Use a call for an ambiguous conversation

    A short conversation can help when several interpretations of the delay remain plausible and the buyer is willing to discuss them. Ask whether the moment is convenient. Prepare one diagnostic question and a brief recap rather than treating the call as permission to restart the entire pitch.

  4. Respect the buyer's channel preference

    If the buyer asks for email, use email. If a call was agreed, keep that agreement rather than replacing it with a surprise sequence. A communication preference is information about how to work together, not an obstacle to overcome through repeated attempts on other channels.

  5. Make the next action proportionate

    The next step might be answering a scope question, including another participant or scheduling a review. Ask for the smallest action that resolves the uncertainty. Do not request a full demonstration when the buyer is still deciding whether the proposed work addresses the problem.

  6. Record a pause honestly

    When the buyer says the project is deferred, record the reason and any mutually agreed date or trigger for reopening it. If there is no agreement to reconnect, do not invent one. Distinguish an active opportunity from a conversation that currently has no next step.

  7. Update the record after contact

    Capture the actual response, the remaining question and any owner and date agreed. Keep unanswered outreach separate from confirmed progress. This makes a later review more useful than a count of calls or emails that says nothing about whether the buyer's situation changed.

Worked example

Illustrative email: 'In our last discussion, the open question was whether implementation includes the existing CRM. The attached scope now names the included work. Is that the remaining issue, or has the priority changed?' If the answer introduces several new constraints, suggest a short call with an agreed purpose.

Your next step

Before the next follow-up, complete three lines: the buyer's last stated position, the unresolved decision and the reason this channel helps. Afterward, record the answer or lack of response without changing the opportunity stage merely because you contacted the buyer.

Common questions

How many follow-ups should I send?

There is no universal count that fits every relationship. Use the prior agreement, relevance of new information and the buyer's stated preferences to decide whether another contact is useful.

Does a short call have to last seven minutes?

Use a short-call structure to stay focused. Let the agreed purpose and the other person's availability determine the appropriate length.
